Terrorism has no religion. Yet, in public discourse, questions often arise: why do certain extremists appear to come predominantly from one faith? Recent incidents, such as the Red Fort blast in Delhi, carried out by educated individuals, have intensified this debate. Observers ask: if terrorism is a product of poverty or lack of education, why do doctors and professionals sometimes engage in violent extremism?

These questions, if poorly framed, can fuel mistrust between communities. It is important to clarify: Islam, like all major religions, is not a religion of extremism. Extremism exists in pockets of every society, faith, and ideology. The real issue is how certain individuals misinterpret religious teachings to justify violence.

This is creating animosity among different religious communities. Is Islam a religion of extremism? How can non-muslims trust muslims if fairly educated people from Islam commit such radicalism? This article is simply one man's opinion. I have provided as much information as I can with my current knowledge and perspective.

---

We need to understand aspects of the holy Qur'an and different schools of thought in Islam and their implications. First of all, the Holy book is prescriptive but often context-dependent. The Qur'an provides principles that require contextualisation, so interpretation is essential.

Examples:

> "And fight in the way of Allah those who fight you but do not transgress. Indeed, Allah does not like transgressors." (Qur'an 2:190)

**Context:** Meant for self-defence during early Islamic battles.

**Exploitation:** Extremists ignore the conditions ("fight those who fight you") and ethical limits, claiming a universal command to attack others.

Or something more pertinent for this article in the Holy book:

> وَلَا تَسْتَوِي الْحَسَنَةُ وَلَا السَّيِّئَةُ ۚ ادْفَعْ بِالَّتِي هِيَ أَحْسَنُ فَإِذَا الَّذِي بَيْنَكَ وَبَيْنَهُ عَدَاوَةٌ كَأَنَّهُ وَلِيٌّ حَمِيمٌ
>
> "And not equal are the good deed and the bad. Repel [evil] by that [deed] which is better; and thereupon the one whom between you and him is enmity [will become] as though he was a devoted friend." (Qur'an 41:34)

**Context:** It draws a clear moral distinction between what is good and what is evil: they are not equal. It instructs believers that when facing wrongdoing (evil), the response should not necessarily mirror that evil, but rather respond with something better (good conduct, kindness, patience, forgiveness, etc.).

**Exploitation:** Because the wording is broad: "repel evil with what is better", different people may interpret "what is better" in very different ways. For some, "better" might mean peaceful patience or forgiveness; for others, misinformed by hate or rage, "better" might be interpreted wrongly as violent retaliation, claiming to "defend" religion. Without context and proper guidance, such verses can be twisted and misapplied.

Now, if contextualisation is important, who gives or defines context? That's the job of Islamic Scholars. There are many schools of thought (Madhhabs) in Islam. Sunni Islam has Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, and Hanbali; Shia Islam has Ja'fari, Ismaili, Zaydi, etc. Schools of thought differ not just in metaphysics, but in practical daily life rules: prayer methods, divorce, inheritance, social behaviour, and even gender interaction norms, with deep methodological reasoning. This may confuse the mind of a layman in Islam about which one is correct and all.

For example:

- **Hanafi:** Reasoning (qiyās) and public interest can be used widely
- **Maliki:** The Practices of the people of Medina are a major source
- **Shafi'i:** Hadith is the primary source after the Qur'an
- **Hanbali:** Very strict reliance on literal texts

All are valid, just different methods. It is due to the widespread nature of the religion with different geolocations and lives. Scholars interpreted the Sunnah (way of life prescribed by the Prophet Muhammad) differently. Even Islamic jurisprudence (fiqh) allows multiple valid interpretations. Now that is beautiful and creates vibrancy; however, Extremists exploit this diversity. They do not acknowledge any other schools of thought or any legitimate scholarship as valid and promote only one kind of narrow-minded, radical misinterpretation of sacred texts, which is less confusing for a layman who knows nothing better.

---

This is more or less known; now comes the question: how come highly educated individuals from well-to-do environments fall prey to this radicalisation?

Radicalisation is not determined by formal education. It is often a matter of identity, belief, and psychological need. Even well-educated individuals may lack deep training in religious texts. When seeking answers:

- They may gravitate toward interpretations that are simple, clear-cut, and emotionally appealing.
- They may ignore the nuanced, historically informed, and context-rich teachings of mainstream scholarship.

In other words, intellect alone cannot shield against radicalisation; proper guidance and critical engagement with faith are crucial. This is one of the many possibilities I can draw.

---

**How to reduce confusion about which school of thought to follow?**

Multiple schools of thought in Islam developed naturally over time due to the need to interpret the Qur'an and Sunnah in diverse contexts. It wasn't about division; it was about understanding and applying Islamic teachings across different cultures, situations, and generations. Understanding these diverse interpretations is especially important in multi-religious societies like India, where misperceptions can exacerbate tension.

For a country like India, where a religion-based bifurcation happened, there is a certain alien feeling for Muslims from Hindus and vice versa in the grassroots. "Oh, these people are different from us." "They see life differently." "Their lifestyle is different from ours." Thoughts like these can be found in both communities for each other. As a non-muslim, I think scholars can think and preach a school of thought from both the Sunni and the Shia branches of Islam, which is apt for a society like India or other societies. All are correct as they are all mutually approved in their respective branches.

**How to stop it and reduce this alien feeling?**

In my humble opinion, social isolation based on religion and limited interaction with people of other religions is a thing of the past. Extremism is not the product of a religion; it is the distortion of it. Misinterpretation, social alienation, and psychological vulnerability, not educational or economic status alone, can lead even educated individuals astray.

The antidote lies in context, scholarship, and community engagement. By fostering understanding across schools of thought and across communities, society can counter extremism, reduce mistrust, and reinforce the true ethical spirit of religion.
